# Write data people would actually say
- Agree language variety and spelling with a competent reviewer. Preserve meaningful diacritics.
- Start from situations, not a list of English sentences to translate word for word.
- Vary requests, vocabulary, order and context. Avoid 25 copies that differ only in names.
- Include both short and longer natural forms. Record genuine code-switching; do not inject it just to inflate variety.
- Use fictitious non-sensitive examples. Do not use real student grades, patient records, private messages or contact details.
- For sentiment, positive means approval ('Huduma ilikuwa nzuri sana.'); negative means disapproval ('Nimesikitishwa na huduma hii.'); neutral has no clear opinion ('Ofisi inafunguliwa saa mbili asubuhi.'). These teaching examples need local fluent review and do not prove clock-time equivalence.
- An enthusiastic weather question is still a weather intent. Sentiment describes attitude, intent describes desired action.
- For semantic parsing, copy slot values exactly from the utterance. Keep relative expressions such as 'kesho' as text. Do not infer an exact date from an unknown context or convert Swahili clock times silently.
- Have someone who did not author the test example judge it. Keep disagreement notes; do not hide difficult cases.
- AI can help explain code or propose wording to review, but bulk synthetic text does not count as independently validated human data.
